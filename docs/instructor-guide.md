# Teaching with The Rate Race

**[Open the game](https://chengyu-ucsb.github.io/The-Rate-Race/)**

The Rate Race asks students to design an organization and then work inside it. Five shifts introduce methods and training, specialization, authority, rules, pay, and a change in demand. Students process six multi-step orders each shift and use their results to examine the assumptions behind classical management.

The central question is: **What becomes easier when work is organized like a machine, and what becomes harder to see or address?**

## Learning goals

After playing, students should be able to:

- Distinguish Taylor's scientific management, Fayol's administrative principles, and Weber's account of bureaucracy.
- Explain how a work method, structure, communication route, or pay rule changes what employees can do.
- Identify benefits of specialization, standardization, and formal rules as well as their limitations.
- Support a claim with a decision and an outcome from their own run.

## Suggested activity

Allow approximately 25–35 minutes for play and discussion, or 15–20 minutes for individual exploration before an asynchronous reflection. These are estimates for planning, not measured completion times. Students can take time to read and decide; the redesigned game charges simulated work time instead of timing their physical responses.

| Stage | Suggested time | Instructor action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Introduce the problem | 3 minutes | Ask what an efficient organization should optimize. Keep students' proposed measures visible. |
| Play five shifts | 15–20 minutes | Students play individually or in pairs on one device. In pairs, alternate the person selecting policies and the person processing orders. |
| Compare evidence | 3–5 minutes | Have students inspect the final report and compare the same orders under a different policy. |
| Discuss | 4–7 minutes | Ask what changed, why it changed, and what the model leaves out. |

Students do not need separate manager and worker websites. The game moves between planning, work, and reports on one page. A pair sharing a device can debate choices; there is no networked multiplayer session.

The result can be copied or printed. Share/resume creates a URL containing a snapshot of the run, which can be reopened on another device. A later change needs a new link. Reflection prompts are for use in Canvas or another submission system; the game does not collect written responses or submit grades.

## How students process an order

The workbench combines an order ticket, a nine-bin shelf, and a packing area. Product illustrations help students recognize what they are handling, but the exact stock code (SKU) determines which variant the customer requested.

1. **Match the full SKU.** Find the exact code on the shelves. Choosing the right product family with the wrong code still produces a picking error.
2. **Select the requested quantity.** Count one, two, or three units into the parcel. The parcel preview reflects the chosen quantity and packaging.
3. **Choose packaging.** A mailer holds one or two nonfragile units. Fragile goods or three-unit orders require a box.
4. **Decide whether to inspect.** Inspection starts unchecked for each order. Selecting it adds simulated time and catches mistakes that require rework. Skipping it can let a wrong item, wrong count, unsuitable package, damaged item, or handling slip reach dispatch.
5. **Choose pace and recovery, then dispatch.** Rushing changes time and fatigue in the model; a recovery break also has a time cost. Feedback distinguishes work completed without correction, problems caught and reworked, late orders, and defects that reached dispatch.

Shelf locations stay fixed within a shift. The first shift has distinct products and one unit per order; the second introduces look-alike variants and quantities of one or two. From the third shift, quantities can reach three, making package capacity another requirement. Difficulty comes from interpreting the order and coordinating decisions, without a real-time countdown.

The warehouse flow display tracks progress through the six orders, and illustrated crew cards accompany staffing decisions. Use the weekly chart to compare on-time good orders, late good orders, and defective shipments; the table provides exact values. The visuals represent the game state rather than a live simulation of parallel production stations.

## What to notice in each shift

### 1. Methods and training: Taylor

Ask students to distinguish choosing a method from performing it. Does a clear procedure reduce uncertainty? What happens when management expects compliance without investing in training? Which aspects of the job can a method comparison actually capture?

Taylor's scientific management includes systematic study of work, selecting workers for jobs, training, and separating management's planning from workers' execution. A stopwatch by itself is not the entire theory. The game's method choices are an opportunity to discuss what would count as adequate evidence for declaring one method best.

**Reading:** Miller (2015), pp. 26–28; Week 2 Mini-lecture 3, slides 4 and 6.

### 2. Division of labor: Fayol

Use worker assignments and the absence event to compare individual expertise with the needs of the whole process. Specialization can develop proficiency while making it harder to move capacity when demand changes. The game represents this interruption through extra handoff time; it does not simulate simultaneous station queues.

Distinguish **unity of command**, receiving instructions from one supervisor for a task, from **unity of direction**, coordinating activities toward a shared objective. A department can meet its own target while making another department's work harder. This is a problem of coordination, not necessarily a lack of effort.

**Reading:** Miller (2015), pp. 19–22. The Creamy Creations case, pp. 35–36, offers a useful follow-up on specialization, bottlenecks, and customer relationships.

### 3. Authority, communication, and rules: Fayol and Weber

Follow an exception through the available communication routes. What information must travel, who can authorize an action, and where does work wait? Contrast a strict scalar chain with an authorized lateral connection.

Fayol's **gangplank** permits communication across the hierarchy when a superior authorizes it and it helps the work. It is not a rejection of organization or responsibility.

In this shift, Sales asks for a favored account to jump the queue. If students resolve the conflict through a recorded escalation or an authorized call, written criteria let them apply the same standard to every account. This reduces the time spent assembling an individual justification and increases modeled trust, while routine documentation still costs time on every order. Written records also make the final shift's quality exception easier to justify.

Ask whether the decision belongs to a powerful individual or to an office applying an established criterion. Weber's rational-legal authority rests on a system of rules and authorized offices, rather than the personal wishes of whoever holds a role. The case gives formal procedures a protective function as well as a cost.

Weber's bureaucracy is an **ideal type**, an analytical model of an organizational form. “Ideal” does not mean morally perfect. Formal procedures can produce reliability and impartiality while still being poorly suited to an unfamiliar case.

**Reading:** Miller (2015), pp. 21 and 24–25; Week 2 Mini-lecture 2, slides 9 and 11–13; classical organization handout, section 5.

### 4. Pay and the moving target

Compare pay tied to simulated work time, a stable piece rate, and a target that changes after above-target performance. Ask what each policy makes rational for an employee. Is apparent low effort always evidence of low motivation, or can it reflect an understandable response to the rules? Pay is expressed in fictional team credits, not real wages.

Do not identify arbitrary rate cuts as Taylor's recommendation. Miller presents **rate-busting** as a practice that helped produce **systematic soldiering**, in which workers limited output to protect earnings. Taylor proposed standards and piece rates established through scientific study as a response to that problem. The moving target illustrates a contrasting incentive system.

**Reading:** Miller (2015), pp. 26–28. Schumpeter's 2015 “Digital Taylorism” is a historical extension to continuous measurement and ranking.

### 5. Changed demand: test the assumptions

Ask students which established rule still helps and which no longer fits. Does the organization have a way to recognize an exception and revise its procedure? Does information reach the person who can act on it?

Checking the final batch addresses damaged stock. It does not prevent a later picking, counting, or packing mistake; those still require attention to the order and, when chosen, an individual inspection. Ask students to distinguish a useful procedure from an assumption that one check guarantees all later work.

Classical communication tends to be task-focused, formal, written, and downward. That does not mean upward messages are impossible. The lecture distinguishes information needed for work, reports of compliance, problems, and suggestions. An organization can formally permit a message while making it slow or difficult to send.

**Reading:** Miller (2015), pp. 30–32; Week 2 Mini-lecture 3, slides 10–17.

## Use the comparison carefully

A controlled replay is available after each shift. The final debrief's comparison replays the last shift with the same orders, work decisions, event response, and starting conditions under a different policy. This helps students trace a model's consequences without also changing the workload. It does not rerun the whole week: training purchased earlier remains available. Ask students to name the policy difference, identify an outcome, and explain the mechanism.

Read the measures separately. The target counts good orders completed before dispatch; a late but correct order is still good output. Rate means good orders per simulated hour. Fatigue and trust are modeled indicators, not measurements of students' bodies or attitudes.

Treat the game's numerical relationships as teaching assumptions. They are not measured effects from Taylor, Fayol, Weber, or the assigned articles. A model can expose a tradeoff without proving that real employees would respond in exactly the same way. Students can also challenge what is measured, whose interests the measures represent, and which consequences the game omits.

Avoid grading output speed or the supposed best policy. Evaluate the quality of students' explanations and their use of evidence and concepts.

## Optional written reflection

Ask students to submit their copied or printed result with a **200–300 word reflection** in Canvas. This is a suggested activity, not a built-in grading policy.

1. Choose one decision from your run. Name the relevant theorist and concept, then explain the outcome using specific evidence from your report.
2. Identify one way the organization benefited from specialization, standardization, or formal authority, and one limitation revealed by an exception or change in demand.
3. Recommend one revision. Explain what it should improve, what it might cost, and what additional evidence you would want from a real workplace.
